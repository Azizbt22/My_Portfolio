01 / Context

Emotion in language is rarely a single-label problem.

Emotion detection can support social-media analysis, human-computer interaction and research on how people communicate. The SemEval shared task provides a common dataset and evaluation protocol for comparing approaches under the same conditions.

During my semester at TU Dresden, I participated in SemEval 2025 Task 11-A as part of a three-person team. My work focused on the model architecture, training experiments and analysis of the final predictions.

Each short text can express several emotions at once. A sentence may communicate fear and sadness together, or joy mixed with surprise. The output is therefore a set of five binary labels, not one mutually exclusive class.

02 / Objective and ML formulation

Predict five labels from a small and imbalanced corpus.

The English dataset contains 2,768 annotated texts. The labels are unevenly distributed: fear appears 1,611 times, while anger appears 333 times. The model must therefore learn from limited supervision without allowing frequent emotions to dominate evaluation.

Fine-tune a pretrained language model for multi-label emotion detection, compare several transformer architectures and select a configuration that performs consistently across all five emotions.

03 / Dataset and preprocessing

Preserve the multi-label targets and test augmentation separately.

I represented each target as a five-position binary vector. Texts were tokenised with the tokenizer associated with each transformer, padded in batches and truncated to a maximum length of 256 tokens.

Translation-based augmentation experiment

The project also tested whether additional translated examples could compensate for the small training set. German SemEval texts were translated into English with DeepL. Examples containing the additional disgust category were removed, and the remaining translated data was added only to the training portion. Validation and test stayed in original English.

Compare contextual encoders before tuning the final system.

RoBERTa-large achieved the strongest average and maximum macro F1, so I retained it for the final experiments. BERT-large was the closest baseline. The smaller architectures reduced computation but left a clear performance gap on this corpus.

I trained the five outputs with binary cross-entropy. This treats each emotion as an independent binary decision and allows co-occurring labels. A softmax layer would force the probabilities to compete and would be incompatible with the task.

05 / Training protocol

Keep validation separate and retain the best checkpoint.

The English corpus was split into 80% training, 10% validation and 10% testing. I used validation performance for checkpoint and threshold selection. The test set remained untouched until the final evaluation.

WEIGHT DECAY0.02
GRADIENT CLIP0.5

SCHEDULERLinear decay

The model was evaluated after every epoch and the checkpoint with the highest validation macro F1 was saved. In the final training run, the selected checkpoint came from epoch five. Training loss continued to fall afterwards while validation loss followed a U-shaped curve, indicating that later epochs mainly increased overfitting.

06 / Evaluation and threshold tuning

Model selection and label decisions require different validation steps.

Macro F1 was the primary metric because it gives equal weight to anger, fear, joy, sadness and surprise. I also monitored micro F1, precision, recall, accuracy and Hamming loss to obtain complementary views of prediction quality.

→
VALIDATESelect checkpoint and thresholds
→
TESTApply the frozen configuration once

The initial decision threshold was 0.5 for every label. I then searched values from 0.1 to 0.9 on validation predictions. The best thresholds differed between emotions, ranging from 0.10 to 0.52 with a mean of 0.32. This reflects differences in label frequency and probability calibration.

MACRO F1

Primary selection metric

Computes F1 separately for each emotion and averages them with equal weight.

MICRO F1

Overall label decisions

Aggregates true and false predictions across every text-label pair.

HAMMING LOSS

Label-level error rate

Measures the proportion of individual emotion decisions that are incorrect.

PRECISION / RECALL

Error trade-off

Shows whether each label is over-predicted or missed too frequently.

08 / Explainability and error analysis

SHAP showed both meaningful cues and brittle shortcuts.

I used SHAP text explanations to inspect which tokens increased or decreased each emotion score. Applying a logit transformation made contributions easier to read in the generated visualisations.

STRONG CASES

Explicit emotional language

Words such as “terrified” or “celebrate” produced clear and interpretable contributions.

AMBIGUITY

Too little situational context

Short statements sometimes supported several plausible interpretations.

IRONY AND SARCASM

Literal wording contradicted intent

The encoder often followed positive surface words in a sentence whose real meaning was negative.

NEGATION

Emotion words dominated local context

Expressions such as “not happy” could still activate joy because the cue received more weight than the negation.

The error analysis suggested different improvements for different failures: more diverse examples for rare expressions, contrastive training for negation, and additional conversational or multimodal context for irony and ambiguity.

09 / Limits and learnings

The experimental protocol mattered as much as the encoder.

Translation quality

The translated examples were not manually assessed, so the precise source of their performance drop remains uncertain.

Dataset size

The test portion is small, which increases uncertainty around label-level conclusions.

Missing context

Some emotional meaning depends on speaker history, culture, tone or situation that the text alone does not contain.

The project taught me to treat data quality, checkpoint selection and decision thresholds as part of the model. A stronger encoder helps, but the final result depends on the complete experimental protocol.
